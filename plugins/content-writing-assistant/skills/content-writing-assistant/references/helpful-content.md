# Google's helpful content guidance, operationalised

Source: Google Search Central, "Creating helpful, reliable, people-first content"
https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content
(page last updated 2026-10-05 when this skill was written; re-check it if the skill is old)

This file paraphrases the guidance into checks you can run. It is used three times per run: as a lens on competitor pages (Step 3), as a gate on the draft (Step 6), and as a reported result (Step 10).

## The core idea

Google's systems aim to reward content made primarily for people, not content made primarily to rank. SEO is fine when it helps people-first content get found and understood. It is a problem when it replaces the people-first purpose. The single most important question is **why** the content exists.

## Self-assessment: content and quality

Answer yes or no for the draft, with a one-line reason. Fix every no.

1. **Original value**: does it add original information, reporting, research, analysis or a worked example, rather than restating what is already out there?
2. **Thoroughness**: does it cover the topic substantially and go past the obvious?
3. **Value over sources**: where it draws on sources, does it add meaningful value instead of rewording them?
4. **Honest headings**: are the title and headings descriptive, without exaggeration or shock value?
5. **Bookmark test**: is this something a reader would save, share or recommend, or that would belong in a respected publication on the subject?
6. **Better than the SERP**: does it offer substantial value compared with the pages already ranking? (Point to the gap sections.)
7. **Production quality**: free of spelling and style errors, and does it read as carefully produced rather than rushed?
8. **Not mass-produced**: does it read as made with care for this audience, not as one interchangeable page among many?

## Self-assessment: expertise and trust

9. **Trust signals**: is it clear who wrote it, what their background is, and where the facts come from (sources, author bio, link to about/author page)?
10. **Reputation**: would someone researching the site or author come away seeing them as a credible source on this topic?
11. **Expert authorship**: written or reviewed by someone with real expertise or deep first-hand knowledge? If not, recommend a reviewer.
12. **Verifiable accuracy**: free of easily checked factual errors? Every statistic sourced or marked [VERIFY]?

## People-first signals (aim for all)

- The intended audience would find it useful if they arrived directly, not via search
- It shows first-hand experience or depth of knowledge (used the product, did the work, handled the case, visited the place)
- It fits the site's established focus
- A reader finishes with enough to reach their goal and does not need to search again

## Search-engine-first warning signs (any one is a flag)

- Written mainly to attract search visits
- Part of a scattershot approach covering many topics in hope some rank
- Heavy automation across many topics with little human input
- Mostly summarises others without adding value
- Chases a trend the audience would not otherwise care about
- Leaves readers needing to search again for a better answer
- **Written to hit a word count.** Google states it has no preferred length
- Enters a niche without real expertise, mainly for traffic
- Promises an answer that does not exist (an unconfirmed date, a guaranteed outcome)
- Changes dates or shuffles content to look fresh without substantive updates

If the brief itself triggers one of these, raise it with the user at intake rather than writing around it.

## E-E-A-T and YMYL

- Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trust. **Trust matters most**; a page does not need all four, but it needs to be trustworthy
- E-E-A-T is not itself a ranking factor; Google uses a mix of signals that align with it
- Topics that can significantly affect health, financial stability, safety or society (YMYL) get more weight on these signals. For YMYL: match expert consensus, cite primary sources, prefer a qualified author or reviewer, and never speculate
- Main content is judged on effort, originality, talent or skill, and accuracy. That includes media, tools and headings, not just body text

## Who, How, Why

- **Who**: visible byline where readers expect one, linking to an author page with real background. Invented authors, AI-generated headshots or false credentials are deceptive
- **How**: explain how the content was produced where readers would care. For reviews and tests: what was tested, how many, what method, with evidence such as original photos. Where automation or AI meaningfully contributed and readers would reasonably wonder, disclose it and explain why it helped
- **Why**: to help people. Using automation primarily to manipulate rankings breaches Google's spam policy on scaled content abuse. This skill is a drafting assistant: a human with real expertise must supply experience, verify facts and approve before publishing. Say so in the open items

## Applying it to competitor pages (Step 3)

For each analyzed page, give a quick read on: original value (yes/partial/no), visible author and credentials, sourcing quality, first-hand experience shown, and any warning signs (padding, fake freshness, exaggerated headings). Weak scores on ranking pages are opportunities: they mark where the new article can be genuinely better, not just longer.
